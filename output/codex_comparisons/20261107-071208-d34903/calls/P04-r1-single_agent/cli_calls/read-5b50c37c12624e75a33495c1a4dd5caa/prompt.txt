L261: Mercury Orbit Insertion & Station Keeping
L262: Getting into Mercury orbit….
L263: On March 18, 2011 UTC (March 17, 2011 EDT), after almost five years in development and more than six and a
L264: half years in cruise toward its destination, NASA’s MErcury Surface, Space ENvironment, GEochemistry, and Ranging
L265: (MESSENGER) spacecraft will execute a 15-minute maneuver that will place it into orbit about Mercury, making it the
L266: first craft ever to do so, and initiating a one-year science campaign to understand the innermost planet. The Mercury
L267: Orbit Insertion maneuver and subsequent orbital activities are described in the next few pages.
L268: Just over 33 hours before the main Mercury orbit insertion event, two antennas from the Deep Space Network —
L269: one main antenna and one backup — will begin to track the MESSENGER spacecraft continuously. Nearly thirty-one
L270: hours later, at 6:30 p.m. EDT on March 17, 2011, the number of antennas tracking MESSENGER will increase to five —
L271: four of these are arrayed together in order to enhance the signal coming from the spacecraft, and a fifth will be used for
L272: backup.
L273: About two and a half hours later, at 8:00 p.m. EDT, the solar arrays, telecommunications, attitude control, and
L274: autonomy systems will all be configured for the main thruster firing (known as a “burn”), and the spacecraft will be
L275: turned into the correct orientation for MESSENGER’s Mercury orbit insertion maneuver.
L276: In order to slow the spacecraft down sufficiently so that it can be captured into orbit around Mercury, the main
L277: thruster will begin firing at 8:45 p.m. and will continue for 15 minutes until 9:00 p.m. About 31% of the spacecraft’s
L278: original allotment of propellant is required for Mercury orbit insertion, and MESSENGER’s thrusters must slow the
L279: spacecraft by just over 0.86 kilometers (0.53 miles) per second. As the spacecraft approaches Mercury, the largest
L280: thruster must fire close to the forward velocity direction of the spacecraft. After the thruster has finished firing, the
L281: spacecraft will be turned toward Earth and reconfigured for normal post-maneuver operations. Data will be collected by
L282: Deep Space Network antennas and transferred to the Mission Operations Center at APL to be analyzed. It is expected
L283: that by 10:00 p.m. EDT the Mission Operations Team will be able to confirm that MESSENGER has been successfully
L284: captured into orbit around Mercury.
L285: Approximately one and a half hours after the maneuver is complete, the DSN coverage will be stepped back to two
L286: stations. At 2:47 a.m. EDT on March 18, the spacecraft will begin its first full orbit around Mercury (as measured from
L287: the highest point in the orbit). About 10 hours later, the Deep Space Network coverage will be further reduced to
L288: continuous coverage with only one station.
L289: The MESSENGER spacecraft will continue to orbit Mercury once every twelve hours for the duration of its primary
L290: mission. The first two weeks from orbit insertion will be focused on ensuring that the spacecraft systems are all working
L291: well in the harsh thermal environment of orbit; this interval is known as the orbital commissioning phase. Starting on
L292: March 23 the instruments will be turned on and checked out, and on April 4 the science phase of the mission will begin
L293: and the first orbital science data from Mercury will be returned.

L371: ….and staying there
L372: After MESSENGER arrives in its primary science orbit, small forces, such as solar gravity — the gravitational attraction
L373: of the Sun — slowly change the spacecraft’s orbit. Although these small forces have little effect on MESSENGER’s
L374: 12-hour orbit period, they can increase the spacecraft’s minimum altitude, orbit inclination, and latitude of the surface
L375: point below MESSENGER’s minimum altitude. Left uncorrected, the increase in the spacecraft’s minimum altitude would
L376: prevent satisfactory completion of several science goals.
L377: To keep the spacecraft’s minimum altitude below 500 kilometers (310 miles), propulsive maneuvers must occur at
L378: least once every Mercury year — one complete revolution around the Sun, or 88 Earth days. The first, third, and fifth
L379: maneuvers after Mercury orbit insertion will occur at the farthest orbital distance from Mercury, where a minimum
L380: amount of propellant will be used to slow the spacecraft just enough to lower the minimum altitude to 200 kilometers
L381: (124 miles). The act of lowering the spacecraft’s altitude in this way has an unavoidable side effect of also lowering orbit
L382: period by about 15 minutes.
L383: The second and fourth maneuvers after orbit insertion will increase the orbit period back to about 12 hours by
L384: speeding up the spacecraft around the time when it is closest to Mercury. Because the sunshade must protect the main
L385: part of the spacecraft from direct sunlight during propulsive maneuvers, the timing of these maneuvers is limited to a
L386: few days when Mercury is either near the same point in its orbit as it was during Mercury orbit insertion, or near the
L387: point where Mercury is on the opposite side of the Sun from that for orbit insertion.

L401: To accomplish these science goals, the MESSENGER spacecraft must obtain many types of observations from different
L402: portions of its orbit around Mercury. Some major constraints must be met, including completing the observations within
L403: two Mercury solar days (equivalent to one Earth year) and keeping the spacecraft sunshade facing the Sun at all times.
L404: The observation plan must also take into account MESSENGER’s orbit around Mercury. The orbit is highly elliptical
L405: (egg-shaped), with the spacecraft passing 200 kilometers (124 miles) above the surface at the lowest point and more
L406: than 15,193 kilometers (9,420 miles) at the highest. At the outset of the orbital phase of the mission, the plane of the
L407: spacecraft’s orbit is inclined 82.5° to Mercury’s equator, and the lowest point in the orbit is reached at a latitude of 60°
L408: North.
L409: The spacecraft’s orbit is elliptical rather than circular because the planet’s surface radiates back heat from the Sun. At
L410: an altitude of 200 km, the re-radiated heat from the planet alone is 4 times the solar intensity at Earth. By spending only
L411: a short portion of each orbit flying this close to the planet, the temperature of the spacecraft can be better regulated.

L413: Observing the surface
L414: MESSENGER’s 12-month orbital phase covers two Mercury solar days; one Mercury solar day, from sunrise to sunrise,
L415: is equal to 176 Earth days. This means that the spacecraft passes over a given spot on the surface only twice during the
L416: mission, 6 months apart, making the time available to observe the planet’s surface a precious resource. The first solar
L417: day is focused on obtaining global map products from the different instruments, and the second focuses on specific
L418: targets of scientific interest and completion of a global stereo map.
L419: As Mercury moves around the Sun, the spacecraft’s orbit around the planet stays in a nearly fixed orientation that
L420: allows MESSENGER to keep its sunshade toward the Sun. In effect, Mercury rotates beneath the spacecraft and the
L421: surface illumination changes with respect to the spacecraft view. At some times, the spacecraft is traveling in an orbit
L422: that follows the terminator — the line that separates day from night. These are known as “dawn-dusk” orbits and are
L423: good for imaging surface features such as craters, as shadows are prominent and topography and texture can be clearly
L424: seen. At other times, the spacecraft follows a path that takes it directly over a fully lit hemisphere of Mercury, then over
L425: a completely dark hemisphere. These are called “noon-midnight” orbits and are good for taking color observations on
L426: the dayside, because there are fewer shadows to obscure surface features.
L427: Some instruments, such as Mercury Laser Altimeter (MLA), can operate whether the surface is lit or not, but others,
L428: such as Mercury Dual Imaging System (MDIS), need sunlight in order to acquire data. The low-altitude segments of
L429: the orbit over the northern hemisphere will allow MESSENGER to conduct a detailed investigation of the geology and
L430: composition of Mercury’s giant Caloris impact basin — the planet’s largest known surface feature, among other goals.
L431: As Mercury moves around the Sun, the MESSENGER spacecraft stays in an approximately fixed
L432: orientation with its sunshade facing the Sun, so effectively the planet rotates beneath the spacecraft.
L433: Different parts of the surface are illuminated depending on where Mercury is in its year, so the
L434: spacecraft can view the surface of the planet under every possible lighting condition.

L436: Orchestrating the observations
L437: Different instruments are given priority in determining spacecraft pointing at different portions of the spacecraft orbit
L438: and as a function of the parts of Mercury’s surface that are illuminated at any given time. For example, MLA “drives”
L439: the spacecraft pointing whenever its laser can range to the planet’s surface (less than ~1500 km altitude), UVVS controls
L440: the pointing when no other instruments can “see” the planet, and MAG and EPPS primarily ride along and collect
L441: data regardless of what else is going on, since they generally don’t need to point at the planet’s surface. The two MDIS
L442: imagers are mounted on a common pivot, and so they can often look at the surface or at other targets when the rest of
L443: the instruments are pointed in a different direction.
L444: MESSENGER will operate in orbit around Mercury for one Earth year, equivalent to four Mercury years or
L445: two Mercury solar days. Different portions of the orbit are used by different instruments to acquire data.

L565: Why Mercury?
L566: Mercury, Venus, Earth, and Mars are the terrestrial (rocky) planets. Among these, Mercury is an extreme: the smallest,
L567: the densest (after correcting for self-compression), the one with the oldest surface, the one with the largest daily
L568: variations in surface temperature, and the least explored. Understanding this “end member” among the terrestrial
L569: planets is crucial to developing a better understanding of how the planets in our Solar System formed and evolved. To
L570: develop this understanding, the MESSENGER mission, spacecraft, and science instruments are focused on answering six
L571: key questions.
L572: Question 1: Why is Mercury so dense?
L573: Each of the terrestrial planets consists of a dense iron-rich core surrounded by a rocky mantle, composed largely of
L574: magnesium and iron silicates. The topmost layer of rock, the crust, formed from minerals with lower melting points than
L575: those in the underlying mantle, either during differentiation early in the planet’s history or by later volcanic or magmatic
L576: activity. The density of each planet provides information about the relative sizes of the iron-rich core and the rocky
L577: mantle and crust, since the metallic core is much denser than the rocky components. Mercury’s uncompressed density
L578: (what its density would be without compaction of its interior by the planet’s own gravity) is about 5.3 g/cm3, by far
L579: the highest of all the terrestrial planets. In fact, Mercury’s density implies that at least 60% of the planet is a metal-rich
L580: core, a figure twice as great as for Earth, Venus, or Mars. To account for about 60% of the planet’s mass, the radius of
L581: Mercury’s core must be approximately 75% of the radius of the entire planet!
L582: There are three major theories to explain why Mercury is so much denser and more metal-rich than Earth, Venus,
L583: and Mars. Each theory predicts a different composition for the rocks on Mercury’s surface. According to one idea,
L584: before Mercury formed, drag by solar nebular gas near the Sun mechanically sorted silicate and metal grains, with the
L585: lighter silicate particles preferentially slowed and lost to the Sun; Mercury later formed from material in this region and
L586: is consequently enriched in metal. This process doesn’t predict any change in the composition of the silicate minerals
L587: making up the rocky portion of the planet, just the relative amounts of metal and rock. In another theory, tremendous
L588: heat in the early nebula vaporized part of the outer rock layer of proto-Mercury and left the planet strongly depleted in
L589: volatile elements. This idea predicts a rock composition poor in easily evaporated elements like sodium and potassium.
L590: The third idea is that a giant impact, after proto-Mercury had formed and differentiated, stripped off the primordial crust
L591: and upper mantle. This idea predicts that the present-day surface is made of rocks highly depleted in those elements
L592: that would have been concentrated in the crust, such as aluminum and calcium.

L594: nebula shows the time when the terrestrial planets
L595: were forming. Processes such as nebular gas drag,
L596: vaporization in the hot early nebula, and giant
L597: impacting collisions have all been suggested as
L598: having possible effects on the bulk composition of

L605: MESSENGER will determine which of these ideas is correct by measuring the composition of the rocky surface. X-ray,
L606: gamma-ray, and neutron spectrometers will measure the elements present in the surface rocks and determine if volatile
L607: elements are depleted or if elements that tend to be concentrated in planetary crusts are deficient. A visible-infrared
L608: spectrometer will determine which minerals are present and will permit the construction of mineralogical maps of the
L609: surface. Analysis of gravity and topography measurements will provide estimates of the thickness of Mercury’s crust. To
L610: make these challenging measurements of Mercury’s surface composition and crustal characteristics, these instruments
L611: will need to accumulate many observations of the surface. MESSENGER’s three Mercury flybys provided opportunities
L612: to make preliminary observations, but numerous measurements from an orbit around Mercury are needed to determine
L613: accurately the surface composition. Once in orbit, these measurements will enable MESSENGER to distinguish among
L614: the different proposed origins for Mercury’s high density and, by doing so, gain insight into how the planet formed and
L615: evolved.
L616: Question 2: What is the geologic history of Mercury?
L617: Prior to MESSENGER, only 45% of Mercury’s surface had been seen by spacecraft during the Mariner 10 mission.
L618: Combining the Mariner 10 photos with the images from MESSENGER’s three Mercury flybys, about 98% of the surface
L619: of Mercury has been seen in detail. It is now possible for the first time to begin to investigate Mercury’s geologic history
L620: on a global basis.
L621: Much of Mercury’s surface appears cratered and ancient, with a resemblance to the surface of Earth’s Moon.
L622: Slightly younger, less cratered plains sit within and between the largest old craters. Many of these plains are volcanic,
L623: on the basis of their age relative to nearby large impact features and other indicators of volcanic activity. Data from
L624: MESSENGER’s flybys indicate that volcanism on Mercury persisted for at least the first half of the planet’s history, and
L625: that the style of volcanism included both effusive and
L626: explosive eruptions.
L627: Mercury’s tectonic history is unlike that of any
L628: other terrestrial planet. On the surface of Mercury,
L629: the most prominent features produced by tectonic
L630: forces are long, rounded, lobate scarps or cliffs, some
L631: over a kilometer in height and hundreds of kilometers
L632: in length. These giant scarps are believed to have
L633: formed as Mercury cooled and the entire planet
L634: contracted on a global scale. Understanding the
L635: formation of these scarps thus provides the potential
L636: to gain insight into the thermal history and interior
L637: structure of Mercury.
L638: Once in orbit, MESSENGER will bring a variety
L639: of investigations to bear on Mercury’s geology in
L640: order to determine the sequence of processes that
L641: have shaped the surface. The X-ray, gamma-ray,
L642: and visible-infrared spectrometers will determine
L643: the elemental and mineralogical makeup of rock
L644: units composing the surface. The cameras will image
L645: Mercury’s surface in color and at a typical imaging
L646: resolution that surpasses that of most Mariner 10
L647: pictures. Nearly all of the surface will be imaged in
L648: stereo to determine the planet’s global topographic
L649: variations and landforms; the laser altimeter
L650: will measure the topography of surface features
L651: even more precisely in the northern hemisphere.

L661: Question 3: What is the nature of Mercury’s magnetic field?
L662: Mercury’s magnetic field and the resulting magnetosphere, produced by the interaction of Mercury’s magnetic field
L663: with the solar wind, are unique in many ways. Perhaps one of the most noteworthy observations about Mercury’s
L664: magnetic field is that the small planet possesses one at all. Mercury’s magnetic field is similar in its “dipole” shape to
L665: Earth’s magnetic field, which resembles the field that would be produced if there was a giant bar magnet at the center
L666: of the planet. In contrast, Venus, Mars, and the Moon do not show evidence for intrinsic dipolar magnetic fields, but the
L667: Moon and Mars have evidence for local magnetic fields centered on different rock deposits.
L668: Earth’s magnetosphere is very dynamic and constantly changes in response to the Sun’s activity, including both solar
L669: storms and more modest changes in the solar wind and interplanetary magnetic field. We see the effects of these
L670: dynamics on the ground as they affect power grids and electronics, causing blackouts and interference with radios and
L671: telephones. Mercury’s magnetosphere was shown by Mariner 10 to experience similar dynamics; understanding those
L672: variations will help us understand the interaction of the Sun with planetary magnetospheres in general.
L673: Although Mercury’s magnetic field is thought to be a miniature version of Earth’s, Mariner 10 didn’t measure
L674: Mercury’s field well enough to characterize it. There was even considerable uncertainty in the strength and source of
L675: the magnetic field after Mariner 10. MESSENGER’s Mercury flybys confirmed that there is a global magnetic field on
L676: Mercury, and that the field has a strong dipolar component nearly aligned with the planet’s spin axis. Mercury’s magnetic
L677: field most likely arises from fluid motions in an outer liquid portion of Mercury’s metal core. There is debate, however,
L678: about the molten fraction of the core as well as whether the field is driven by compositional or thermal differences.
L679: These different ideas for the driving force behind Mercury’s magnetic field predict slightly different field geometries, so
L680: careful measurements by spacecraft can distinguish among current theories.

L707: Question 4: What is the structure of Mercury’s core?
L708: As discussed in Questions 1 and 3, Mercury has a very large iron-rich core and a global magnetic field; this
L709: information was first gathered by the Mariner 10 flybys. More recently, Earth-based radar observations of Mercury have
L710: also determined that at least a portion of the large metal core is still liquid. Having at least a partially molten core means
L711: that a very small but detectable variation in the spin rate of Mercury has a larger amplitude because of decoupling
L712: between the solid mantle and the liquid core. Knowing that the core has not completely solidified, even as Mercury has
L713: cooled over billions of years since its formation, places important constraints on the planet’s thermal history, evolution,
L714: and core composition.
L715: However, these constraints are limited because of the low precision of current information on Mercury’s gravity field
L716: from the Mariner 10 and MESSENGER flybys. Fundamental questions about Mercury’s core remain to be explored, such
L717: as its composition. A core of pure
L718: iron would be completely solid
L719: today, due to the high melting
L720: point of iron. However, if other
L721: elements, such as sulfur, are also
L722: present in Mercury’s core, even
L723: at only a level of a few percent,
L724: the melting point is lowered
L725: considerably, allowing Mercury’s
L726: core to remain at least partially
L727: molten as the planet cooled.
L728: Constraining the composition
L729: of the core is intimately tied to
L730: understanding what fraction of
L731: the core is liquid and what fraction
L732: has solidified. Is there just a very
L733: thin layer of liquid over a mostly
L734: solid core, or is the core completely
L735: molten? Addressing questions
L736: such as these can also provide
L737: insight into the current thermal
L738: state of Mercury’s interior, which
L739: is very valuable information for
L740: determining the evolution of the
L741: planet.
L742: Using the laser altimeter in orbit, MESSENGER will verify the presence of a liquid outer core by measuring Mercury’s
L743: libration. Libration is the slow, 88-day wobble of the planet about its rotational axis. The libration of the rocky outer
L744: part of the planet will be twice as large if it is floating on a liquid outer core than if it is frozen to a solid core. By radio
L745: tracking of the spacecraft in orbit, MESSENGER will also determine the gravity field with much better precision than can
L746: be accomplished during flybys. The libration experiment, when combined with improved measurements of the gravity
L747: field, will provide information on the size and structure of the core.
L748: Question 5: What are the unusual materials at Mercury’s poles?
L749: Mercury’s axis of rotation is oriented nearly perpendicular to the planet’s orbit, so that in polar regions sunlight strikes
L750: the surface at a near-constant grazing angle. Some of the interiors of large craters at the poles are thus permanently
L751: shadowed and perpetually very cold. Earth-based radar images of the polar regions show that the floors of large craters
L752: The radius of the core of Mercury is approximately 75% of that of the entire planet,
L753: which is a much larger fraction of the plan than for Earth. Like Earth, Mercury has a
L754: core that is at lest partially liquid. However, unlike Earth, the size of the solid inner
L755: core is not known. (Courtesy of NASA, JHU/APL, CIW.)
L756: 
L757: are highly reflective at radar wavelengths, unlike the surrounding terrain. Furthermore, the radar-bright regions are
L758: consistent in their radar properties with the polar cap of Mars and the icy moons of Jupiter, suggesting that the material
L759: concentrated in the shadowed craters is water ice. The idea of water ice being stable on the surface of the planet closest
L760: to the Sun is intriguing.
L761: The temperature inside these permanently shadowed craters is believed to be low enough to allow water ice to
L762: be stable for the majority of the observed deposits. Ice from infalling comets and meteoroids could be cold-trapped
L763: in Mercury’s polar deposits over millions to billions of years, or water vapor might outgas from the planet’s interior
L764: and freeze at the poles. A few craters at latitudes as low as 72° N have also been observed to contain radar-bright
L765: material in their interiors, and at these warmer latitudes, maintaining stable water ice for longer periods of time may
L766: be more difficult; a recent comet impact, in the last few million years, may be required to satisfy all radar observations.
L767: Alternatively, it has been suggested that the radar-bright deposits are not water ice but rather consist of a different
L768: material, such as sulfur. Sulfur would be stable in the cold traps of the permanently shadowed crater interiors, and the
L769: source of sulfur could be either meteoritic material or the surface of Mercury itself. It has also been proposed that the
L770: naturally occurring silicates that make up the surface of Mercury could produce the observed radar reflections when
L771: maintained at the extremely low temperatures present in the permanently shadowed craters.
L772: MESSENGER’s three flybys of Mercury passed nearly over the equator and did not allow for viewing of the planet’s
L773: poles. Once in orbit around Mercury, however, MESSENGER’s neutron spectrometer will search for hydrogen in any polar
L774: deposits, the detection of which would suggest that the polar deposits are water-rich. The ultraviolet spectrometer and
L775: energetic particle and plasma spectrometer will search for the signatures of hydroxide or sulfur in the tenuous vapor over
L776: the deposits. The laser altimeter will provide information about the topography of the permanently shadowed craters.

L783: Question 6: What volatiles are important at Mercury?
L784: Mercury is surrounded by an extremely thin envelope of gas. It is so thin that, unlike the atmospheres of Venus, Earth,
L785: and Mars, the molecules surrounding Mercury don’t collide with each other and instead bounce from place to place on
L786: the surface like many rubber balls. This tenuous atmosphere is called an “exosphere.”
L787: Seven elements are known to exist in Mercury’s exosphere: hydrogen, helium, oxygen, sodium, potassium, calcium,
L788: and, as discovered by MESSENGER, magnesium. The observed exosphere is not stable on timescales comparable to the
L789: age of Mercury, and so there must be sources for each of these elements. High abundances of hydrogen and helium are
L790: present in the solar wind, the stream of hot, ionized gas emitted by the Sun. The other elements are likely from material
L791: impacting Mercury, such as micrometeoroids or comets, or directly from Mercury’s surface rocks. Several different
L792: processes may have put these elements into the exosphere, and each process yields a different mix of the elements:
L793: vaporization of rocks by impacts, evaporation of elements from the rocks in sunlight, sputtering by solar wind or
L794: magnetospheric ions, or diffusion from the planet’s interior. Strong variability in the composition of Mercury’s exosphere
L795: has been observed, suggesting an interaction of several of these processes.
L796: MESSENGER will determine the composition of Mercury’s exosphere using its ultraviolet spectrometer and energetic
L797: particle and plasma spectrometer. The exosphere composition measured by these instruments will be compared with
L798: the composition of surface rocks measured by the X-ray, gamma-ray, and neutron spectrometers. As MESSENGER orbits
L799: Mercury, variations in the exosphere’s composition will be monitored. The combination of these measurements will
L800: elucidate the nature of Mercury’s exosphere and the processes that contribute to it.
L801: During MESSENGER’s first flyby of Mercury, the distribution of neutral sodium in the
L802: “tail” of Mercury’s exosphere was measured. (Courtesy of NASA, JHU/APL, CIW.)

L804: Highlights from the Mercury Flybys
L805: MESSENGER has completed three flybys of Mercury, utilizing the planet’s gravity to alter its trajectory and bring its
L806: orbit about the Sun closer to that of the innermost planet. All three flybys were executed flawlessly.
L807: To make full use of this opportunity, the Science Team developed a comprehensive plan to conduct observations
L808: throughout the encounters. The data recorded by MESSENGER during its flybys have been used to fine-tune the
L809: observation strategy for the prime orbital phase of the mission. Even now these data are providing exciting new insights
L810: into the history and dynamics of our Solar System’s innermost planet.
L811: MESSENGER’s first flyby of Mercury on January 14, 2008, was a resounding success. Measurements were made that
L812: had never been possible with Mariner 10 or from ground-based telescopes. These include plasma ion measurements,
L813: laser altimetry, high-resolution surface spectroscopy, spacecraft elemental chemical remote sensing, high-spatialresolution observations of both known and new species in Mercury’s exosphere, and eleven-color imaging. In addition
L814: to these observations, complementary measurements were made to those from Mariner 10 by the MESSENGER
L815: Magnetometer, and 21% of the previously unseen hemisphere was imaged for the first time, bringing to 66% the total
L816: surface area of the planet imaged by spacecraft.
L817: During MESSENGER’s second flyby on October 6, 2008, MDIS images filled in a further 24% of the previously unseen
L818: hemisphere, so that 90% of the planet had at that point been observed by spacecraft. Much of the hemisphere imaged
L819: by Mariner 10 was viewed by MESSENGER under different lighting conditions or in color, allowing new discoveries.
L820: MESSENGER became the first spacecraft to fly over the planet’s western hemisphere, making the first measurements of
L821: Mercury’s internal magnetic field above that portion of the planet. All instruments took data during the flyby, and an
L822: emerging picture of Mercury’s global environment and history continued to take shape.
L823: On September 29, 2009, MESSENGER flew by Mercury for the third and final time prior to orbit insertion in March
L824: 2011. An additional 6% of the surface was imaged, completing the equatorial coverage by spacecraft and leaving only
L825: the polar regions yet to be seen by spacecraft. Shortly before closest approach to the planet, as the spacecraft passed
L826: into eclipse, an unexpected configuration of the power system caused the fault protection system to halt the science
L827: command sequence. Although the spacecraft was never at risk and continued through the needed gravity assist, a
L828: number of planned science observations were not made. Despite the truncated set of measurements, new discoveries
L829: were made about the innermost planet, including the first observations of emission from an ionized species in Mercury’s
L830: exosphere, indications of a surprisingly complex distribution of exospheric species over the north and south poles, new
L831: information about magnetic substorms, and evidence for younger volcanism than had been previously anticipated.

L849: Mercury — in color!
L850: During the flybys, the Mercury Dual Imaging System (MDIS) Wide-Angle Camera (WAC) snapped images of Mercury
L851: through 11 different narrow-band color filters, which range from violet in the visible (395 nm) to the near-infrared (1040
L852: nm). The specific colors of the filters were selected to discriminate among common minerals, and statistical methods
L853: that utilize all 11 filters in the visible and near-infrared are used to enhance subtle color differences and aid geologists
L854: in mapping regions of different composition. What do the exaggerated colors tell us about Mercury? The nature of
L855: color boundaries, color trends, and brightness values help MESSENGER geologists understand the distinct regions (or
L856: geological “units”) on the surface. This color information has shown Mercury’s surface to be composed of a variety of
L857: materials with different color characteristics, such as smooth volcanic plains; darker material excavated from depth by
L858: impact craters; younger, less space-weathered material; reddish deposits near volcanic vents; and very bright material on
L859: some crater floors. The color images are complemented by images from the MDIS Narrow-Angle Camera (NAC), which
L860: provides higher resolution views of areas with interesting color properties.
L861: These images are orthographic projections of Mercury created with WAC enhanced-color
L862: images. The orthographic projection produces a view that has the perspective that one
L863: would see from deep space. The WAC enhanced color uses a statistical analysis of images
L864: from all 11 WAC filters to highlight subtle differences in the color of crustal rocks on
L865: Mercury’s surface. The top view uses images from Mercury flyby 1, with the thin crescent
L866: of Mercury imaged during approach forming the right portion of the globe and the fuller
L867: departure view showing Caloris basin forming the left side and majority of the view. The

L873: From the color images alone it is not possible to determine unambiguously the minerals that comprise the rocks
L874: of each unit. During the brief flybys, MESSENGER’s other instruments sensitive to composition lacked the time
L875: needed to build up adequate signal or gain broad areal coverage, so only MESSENGER’s cameras were able to acquire
L876: comprehensive measurements. Once in orbit about Mercury, MESSENGER’s full suite of instruments will be brought to
L877: bear on the newly discovered color units, and the results will provide information about Mercury’s composition and the
L878: processes that acted on Mercury’s surface.
L879: First clues to mineralogy: Clear differences from Earth’s Moon
L880: High-resolution, ultraviolet-to-infrared spectra of Mercury’s surface acquired by the Mercury Atmospheric and Surface
L881: Composition Spectrometer (MASCS) revealed differences in color from the Earth’s Moon that, despite some general
L882: similarities, indicate a different composition. For mineralogical identification, spectral differences of a few percent are
L883: significant, and the differences found by MASCS are up to 20%, indicating that the surface of Mercury has a number of
L884: important surprises in store. Identifying the classes of minerals consistent with the observed spectra will require extensive
L885: analysis and comparison with the color imaging from MDIS and laboratory measurements of the reflectance of mineral
L886: and rock mixtures, but it is already clear that these spectra will play a key role in sifting out the geologic history of the
L887: range of materials evident on the surface and, ultimately, in telling the story of Mercury’s unique history.

L895: far the material fell from the source vent, scientists can estimate the speed at which it was erupted, and what kinds

L952: The duration of volcanic activity
L953: Prior to MESSENGER, it was thought that the volcanism on Mercury had ceased relatively early in its history,
L954: probably around 3.8 billion years ago. Images from MESSENGER’s flybys showed basins that were very sparsely cratered
L955: compared to some other terrains on the planet, implying that they are much younger. On the inner floor of one basin,
L956: Rachmaninoff, there is a volcanic deposit that contains even fewer craters per area than the remaining portions of the
L957: basin floor, suggesting modification by volcanism some time after the basin had formed. By counting the craters per area
L958: in the inner floor deposit, the MESSENGER Science Team has estimated that the volcanic deposit is probably younger
L959: then 2 billion years. Volcanism on Mercury thus continued over a much longer time span than was previously thought,
L960: probably at least half of the history of the Solar System. This discovery means that Mercury’s interior remained hotter for
L961: longer than had been predicted. Once in orbit, other examples of relatively young volcanism may be discovered, and it
L962: will be possible to reconstruct Mercury’s surface history in greater detail.
L963: The great Caloris impact basin
L964: It was known from Mariner 10 photos that Mercury’s Caloris basin is a large, well preserved impact basin, but
L965: MESSENGER images showed the true extent of the feature for the first time. From Mariner 10 photos, only a portion
L966: of the eastern half of Caloris was visible, and the diameter of Caloris was estimated at 1,300 km. MESSENGER’s
L967: images of the entire Caloris basin show that the structure is larger than previously believed, with a diameter of about
L968: 1,550 km.	The	density	of	superposed	smaller	craters	inside	and	outside	Caloris	basin	shows	that	the	deposits	formed	at
L969: the same time as the basin date from fairly early in the history of the Solar System, likely around 3.8 billion years ago.
L970: Plains interior and exterior to the basin, however, have a lower density of impact craters, indicating that they postdate
L971: the basin and consist of volcanic deposits. Near the center of Caloris basin, a set of over 200 narrow troughs, named
L972: Pantheon Fossae, radiate outward in a pattern unlike anything previously seen on Mercury. Structures interpreted
L973: as volcanic vents are seen around the margins of the great basin. Craters with intriguing dark- and light-color
L974: characteristics are found on the basin floor. Overall, understanding the formation and evolution of this giant basin will
L975: provide insight into the early history of major impacts in the inner Solar System, with implications not just for Mercury,
L976: but for all the rocky planets, including Earth.

L993: High-precision topography from laser ranging
L994: The very first laser ranging by the Mercury Laser Altimeter (MLA) to Mercury’s surface yielded topographic profiles
L995: across multiple craters, smooth plains, and other terrain. During the first flyby, MLA was within range only over the night
L996: side of Mercury, and the surface within view of MLA had not been imaged by Mariner 10, so the ranging results were
L997: correlated with Earth-based, radar images of the surface. During the second flyby, topographic measurements were
L998: made across territory that was photographed in high resolution by the MDIS NAC as well as other areas that had been
L999: imaged during the first flyby.
L1000: The MLA ranging provided the first definitive observations of terrain slopes and crater depths on Mercury, showing
L1001: that the slopes are more gradual and older craters shallower than those on the Moon, presumably the result of volcanic
L1002: infilling. The results also clearly show that there is great variation in the surface roughness of crater floors, suggesting
L1003: differences in ages or in the geologic processes that have operated in different craters. From orbit, MESSENGER will be
L1004: able to construct a topographic map of Mercury’s northern hemisphere, enabling a better understanding of the shape
L1005: and depth of impact craters and how they vary on Mercury compared with other bodies.

L1012: Rembrandt — A newly discovered impact basin
L1013: Impact basins are formed by the impact of objects much larger than those that form craters, resulting in much
L1014: larger structures as well as multiple rings of elevated terrain formed during the impact process. MESSENGER’s second
L1015: flyby revealed a basin not previously known, Rembrandt, which has a diameter of 715 km (440 miles). The number
L1016: of impact craters superposed on Rembrandt’s rim indicates that it is one of the youngest basins on Mercury. The floor

L1020: of relationships among the different features within Rembrandt, the relative timing of volcanism, deformation, and
L1021: cratering within this basin is being revealed.

L1104: Solar wind control of Mercury’s magnetosphere
L1105: The Sun has been relatively quiet during and between MESSENGER’s three transits of Mercury’s magnetosphere,
L1106: and the maximum measured strength of Mercury’s internal magnetic field was comparable during each of the flybys.
L1107: Nonetheless, magnetospheric activity varied greatly from
L1108: one flyby to the next as a result of small differences in
L1109: the interplanetary magnetic field (IMF). The north-south
L1110: component of the field outside the magnetosphere, in the
L1111: solar wind, was northward for flyby 1, southward for flyby
L1112: 2, and varied from northward to southward and back during
L1113: flyby 3. The northward IMF during the first flyby produced
L1114: a very quiet magnetosphere, and MESSENGER measured
L1115: steady magnetic fields and registered the presence of only
L1116: very-low-energy charged particles.

L1118: magnetosphere whose outer boundary was highly porous to
L1119: solar wind charged particles as a consequence of magnetic
L1120: reconnection between interplanetary and planetary
L1121: magnetic fields. Huge bundles of twisted magnetic flux,
L1122: somewhat resembling flux bundles ejected from the Sun
L1123: in coronal mass ejections following solar flares, were
L1124: observed to emanate from Mercury’s magnetosphere. This
L1125: dynamic interaction creates magnetic linkage over the polar
L1126: regions of the planet and provides “open windows” for
L1127: the entry of solar wind charged particles. Once inside the
L1128: magnetosphere, these charged particles impact the surface
L1129: of Mercury where they give up their energy to atoms,
L1130: such as sodium, which are ejected to resupply the planet’s
L1131: atmosphere.

L1156: magnetosphere responds more strongly to the direction of the IMF than that of any other planet with an internal
L1157: magnetic field. MESSENGER measurements collected from orbit will be necessary to resolve the question of why
L1158: Mercury’s magnetic fields are so dynamic.
L1159: MESSENGER also made the first measurements of the planetary ions that interact with Mercury’s magnetosphere,
L1160: revealing a remarkable richness in the species present and providing information about the complex interaction of
L1161: the plasma, solar wind, and surface. In addition to the solar wind protons that make up the bulk of the solar wind,
L1162: MESSENGER’s Fast Imaging Plasma Spectrometer discovered that Mercury’s magnetosphere is host to a wide variety
L1163: of heavy ions. This richness of ion species links to material driven off the surface, providing another opportunity for
L1164: deducing, albeit indirectly, Mercury’s surface composition.
L1165: Mercury’s exosphere as never seen before…
L1166: Mercury’s exosphere — an atmosphere so tenuous that particles are more likely to hit the surface than to collide
L1167: with each other — was discovered during the Mariner 10 flybys. Ground-based telescopic observations over the past
L1168: 25 years have added to our knowledge and shown that Mercury also has an extended exospheric tail, a result of solar
L1169: radiation pressure effects (basically sunlight pushing exospheric atoms in the antisunward direction). But these groundbased observations are both difficult to make and limited by the Earth’s atmosphere. With the Mercury Atmospheric and
L1170: Surface Composition Spectrometer (MASCS) on MESSENGER, however, Mercury’s exosphere has been observed with
L1171: unprecedented wavelength coverage and spatial resolution.
L1172: During the three flybys, the Ultraviolet and Visible Spectrometer (UVVS) channel of MASCS obtained the most
L1173: detailed measurements of the exosphere and tail ever made, yielding “maps” of emission from several species present in
L1174: the exosphere, including sodium, calcium, and magnesium. Although both sodium and calcium in Mercury’s exosphere
L1175: had previously been observed with ground-based telescopes on Earth, the flybys were the first time that measurements
L1176: of the two species were obtained simultaneously. Observations of magnesium were a first for MESSENGER because
L1177: the emission from magnesium atoms occurs at ultraviolet wavelengths and is therefore blocked from ground-based
L1178: observation by Earth’s atmosphere.
L1179: Atoms in Mercury’s exosphere that are heavier than hydrogen and helium predominantly originate from the surface
L1180: of Mercury. The detection of magnesium in the exosphere thus provided evidence that magnesium is an important
L1181: component of surface material, something that had long been expected but never proven. A number of processes
L1182: contribute to the release of exospheric species from the surface, and differences in their distributions in both time and
L1183: space provide insight into the relative importance of the processes that generate and maintain the exosphere. The
L1184: observed spatial distributions for all three species differ from one another, indicating that the source and loss processes
L1185: controlling the distributions affect each species in distinct ways or that there are other, currently unknown, processes
L1186: that play a role.

L1256: … complete with “seasonal” variations …
L1257: The emissions observed by the UVVS are primarily solar resonance lines, so-called because the atoms in Mercury’s
L1258: exosphere absorb sunlight at specific wavelengths and then re-radiate a fraction of that light back at the same
L1259: wavelengths. However, the elements in Mercury’s exosphere are also present in the Sun, where they absorb light at these
L1260: same resonance wavelengths and create dark lines in the solar spectrum known as Fraunhofer lines. When Mercury is
L1261: farthest or closest to the Sun in its orbit (aphelion and perihelion, respectively), the planet’s radial velocity with respect
L1262: to the Sun is zero, causing the resonance wavelengths of the atoms to match up with the Fraunhofer lines in the solar
L1263: spectrum, and there is relatively little sunlight for the atoms to absorb and re-emit. For a similar reason, the effects of
L1264: solar radiation pressure are also at a minimum, so there are very few atoms being accelerated into the tail region at
L1265: these same points in Mercury’s orbit. But Mercury’s orbit is elliptical, and when Mercury — along with its surrounding
L1266: exospheric atoms — is accelerating away from or toward the Sun, the heliocentric radial velocity is non-zero and leads
L1267: to a small Doppler shift of sunlight that is just enough to move the atoms’ resonance wavelengths away from the center
L1268: of the deep Fraunhofer lines, providing much more sunlight for the atoms to absorb and re-emit while at the same time
L1269: leading to a larger solar radiation pressure that pushes an increased number of atoms into the tail region.
L1270: Although these changes in the exosphere with Mercury’s orbital position were not unexpected, the MESSENGER
L1271: flybys provided strong evidence for such effects. During the first and second flybys, the sodium tail was both extended
L1272: and bright because the flybys occurred near points in Mercury’s orbit where the Doppler shift was close to the maximum
L1273: and the radiation pressure was high. However, the third flyby occurred at a point where the Doppler shift — and
L1274: therefore the radiation pressure — was small; thus, the tail was effectively “missing,” with the emission down by a
L1275: factor of 10-20 at the same downtail distance compared with the first two flybys. Because radiation pressure affects
L1276: different atoms to different degrees (e.g., sodium strongly, calcium weakly, magnesium insignificantly), the sodium
L1277: tail shows more variation with orbital position than those for other species. The changes in the observed emission as
L1278: Mercury orbits the Sun thus reflect “seasonal-style” variations in Mercury’s exosphere and demonstrate why Mercury’s
L1279: exosphere is perhaps the most dynamic in the solar system. Studying the changes of the “seasons” for a range of
L1280: species during MESSENGER’s orbital mission phase will be vital to quantifying the processes at work in Mercury’s
L1281: exosphere and understanding the transport of volatile material within Mercury’s environment.
L1282: This figure illustrates the “seasonal” variation in Mercury’s neutral sodium tail. The sodium tail at the time of the second
L1283: flyby was well developed whereas the tail was effectively “missing” during the third flyby. Stretching the scale on the third

L1287: maximum radiation pressure, whereas the third flyby occurred close to the point of minimum radiation pressure.

L1305: … and a few surprises
L1306: Whereas the “seasonal” changes in the exosphere observed by MESSENGER were expected, there were a number of
L1307: discoveries during the flybys that were complete surprises.
L1308: During each flyby, a spacecraft roll was executed as MESSENGER entered Mercury’s shadow. This roll rotated the
L1309: look direction of the UVVS field of view about the Sun-Mercury line, carrying it from dawn through north to dusk in
L1310: the first two flybys and from south through dawn to just past north in the third flyby. In all three flybys, an equatorial
L1311: enhancement in the calcium distribution was seen in the dawn direction. This enhancement remains a mystery, not
L1312: just in terms of how it originates but also in its persistence across all three flybys. Despite differences in the space
L1313: environment among flybys that should have affected the source processes for calcium as they are currently understood,
L1314: this feature of the calcium distribution remained relatively unchanged in both location and intensity.
L1315: Other surprises came from the third flyby, which presented a special opportunity to observe the exosphere over the
L1316: polar regions. Altitude profiles of sodium, calcium, and magnesium were obtained over both the north and south poles.
L1317: Although it had been inferred from ground-based observations that sodium is released from the surface by both lowand high-energy processes, the clear two-component nature of the observed altitude profiles for sodium were the first
L1318: direct evidence that this is indeed the case. But more surprising were the profiles for magnesium. A distinctly different
L1319: profile was observed in the north compared to the south, and the structure in the northern profile cannot be fit with
L1320: the normal models applied to Mercury’s exosphere. This difference is even more startling when one considers the fact
L1321: that the calcium profiles show no such north-south difference. Both calcium and magnesium are refractory elements
L1322: and as such are expected to derive from similar processes in approximately the same manner. This is clearly not the case
L1323: over the north pole, and given the lack of an equatorial dawn enhancement in magnesium, the assumption that calcium
L1324: and magnesium will behave similarly is one that must be abandoned in favor of new ideas about the effect of several
L1325: contributory processes.
L1326: Perhaps the biggest surprise during the flybys, though, was the distribution of emission from ionized calcium. The
L1327: observation itself was the first observation of emission from an ionized species in Mercury’s exosphere. The ionized
L1328: calcium was observed to be concentrated close to equatorial plane in a narrow region 1–2 planetary radii tailward of
L1329: the planet. This distribution is inconsistent with local conversion of neutral calcium to ionized calcium, because there
L1330: is simply too much ionized calcium. Instead, the ionized calcium must be concentrated in that region by interaction
L1331: of the calcium ions with Mercury’s magnetosphere. The most likely explanation is that ions created on the dayside of
L1332: the planet, where there should be an abundance of photoions (ions created by sunlight hitting the neutral calcium
L1333: atoms), have been transported around Mercury to the tail region and concentrated by magnetospheric forces. The
L1334: magnetosphere was particularly active during the third flyby, so it remains to be seen whether this concentration was
L1335: specific to the conditions of the third flyby or will be observed during the orbital phase to be a regular phenomenon.
L1336: But one thing is clear: there is a greater degree of interaction between the exosphere and the magnetosphere than
L1337: previously suspected.

L1400: The Spacecraft
L1401: After Mariner 10’s visits to Mercury, the space science and engineering communities yearned for a longer and
L1402: more detailed look at the innermost planet — but that closer look, ideally from orbit, presented formidable technical
L1403: obstacles. A Mercury orbiter would have to be tough, with enough protection to withstand searing sunlight and roasting
L1404: heat bouncing back from the planet below. The spacecraft would need to be lightweight, since most of its mass would
L1405: be fuel to fire its rockets to slow the spacecraft down enough to be captured by Mercury’s gravity. And the probe would
L1406: have to be sufficiently compact to be launched on a conventional and cost-effective rocket.
L1407: Designed and built by the Johns Hopkins University Applied Physics Laboratory (APL) — with contributions from
L1408: research institutions and companies around the world — the MESSENGER spacecraft tackles each of these challenges.
L1409: A ceramic-fabric sunshade, heat radiators, and a mission design that limits time over the planet’s hottest regions protect
L1410: MESSENGER without expensive and impractical cooling systems. The spacecraft’s graphite composite structure — strong,
L1411: lightweight, and heat tolerant — is integrated with a low-mass propulsion system that efficiently stores and distributes
L1412: the approximately 600 kg of propellant that accounts for 54% of the total launch weight.
L1413: To fit behind the 2.5-m by 2-m sunshade, the wiring, electronics, systems, and instruments are packed into a small
L1414: frame that could fit inside a large sport utility vehicle. And the entire spacecraft is light enough to launch on a Delta II
L1415: 7925H-9.5 (“heavy”) rocket, the largest launch vehicle allowed under NASA’s Discovery Program of lower-cost space
L1416: science missions. MESSENGER spacecraft during integration and testing. The sunshade is visible on the left with the
L1417: spacecraft undergoing testing in thermal-vacuum conditions at NASA’s Goddard Space Flight Center. On
L1418: the right, the solar panels and Magnetometer boom are in their position for flight during work at APL.

L1420: Science payload
L1421: MESSENGER carries seven scientific instruments and a radio science experiment to accomplish an ambitious objective:
L1422: return the first data from Mercury orbit. The miniaturized payload — designed to work in the extreme environment
L1423: near the Sun — will image the entire surface of Mercury, as well as gather data on the composition and structure of
L1424: Mercury’s crust, its geologic history, the nature of its active magnetosphere and thin atmosphere, and the makeup of its
L1425: core and the materials near its poles.
L1426: The instruments include the Mercury Dual Imaging System (MDIS), the Gamma-Ray and Neutron Spectrometer
L1427: (GRNS), the X-Ray Spectrometer (XRS), the Magnetometer (MAG), the Mercury Laser Altimeter (MLA), the Mercury
L1428: Atmospheric and Surface Composition Spectrometer (MASCS), and the Energetic Particle and Plasma Spectrometer
L1429: (EPPS). The instruments communicate to the spacecraft through fully redundant Data Processing Units (DPUs).
L1430: The process of selecting the scientific instrumentation for a mission is typically a balance between answering as
L1431: many science questions as possible and fitting within the available mission resources for mass, power, mechanical
L1432: accommodation, schedule, and cost. In the case of MESSENGER, the mass and mechanical accommodation issues were
L1433: very significant constraints. Payload mass was limited to 50 kg because of the propellant mass needed for orbit insertion.
L1434: The instrument mechanical accommodation was difficult because of the unique thermal constraints faced during the
L1435: mission; instruments had to be mounted where Mercury would be in view but the Sun would not, and they had to be
L1436: maintained within an acceptable temperature range in a very harsh environment. Instrument details follow. In each case

L1595: Spacecraft systems and components
L1596: Thermal Design
L1597: While orbiting Mercury, MESSENGER will “feel” significantly hotter than spacecraft that orbit Earth. This is because
L1598: Mercury’s elongated orbit swings the planet to within 46 million km of the Sun, or about two-thirds closer to the Sun
L1599: than Earth. As a result, the Sun shines up to 11 times brighter at Mercury than we see from our own planet.
L1600: MESSENGER’s first line of thermal defense is a heat-resistant and highly reflective sunshade, fixed on a titanium frame
L1601: to the front of the spacecraft. Measuring about 2.5 m tall and 2 m across as viewed head-on, the thin shade has front
L1602: and back layers of Nextel ceramic cloth — the same material that protects sections of the Space Shuttle — surrounding
L1603: several inner layers of Kapton plastic insulation. While temperatures on the front of the shade could reach 370° C when
L1604: Mercury is closest to the Sun, behind it the spacecraft will operate at room temperature, around 20° C.
L1605: As the second line of defense against this challenging environment, the science orbit is designed to limit
L1606: MESSENGER’s exposure to the heat re-radiating from the surface of Mercury. (MESSENGER will only spend about 25
L1607: minutes of each 12-hour orbit crossing Mercury’s broiling surface at low altitude.) Multilayered insulation covers most of
L1608: MESSENGER spacecraft in flight configuration, rear view
L1609: 
L1610: the surfaces of the spacecraft to protect against incident thermal radiation and insulate against internal heat loss, and
L1611: radiators connected to diode (“one-way”) heat pipes are installed on the sides of the spacecraft to carry heat away from
L1612: the spacecraft body. The combination of the sunshade, unique orbital design, thermal blanketing, and heat-radiation
L1613: system allows the spacecraft to operate without special high-temperature electronics.
L1614: Power
L1615: Two single-sided solar panels are the spacecraft’s main source of electric power. To run MESSENGER’s systems and
L1616: charge its 23-ampere-hour nickel-hydrogen battery, the panels, each about 1.5 m by 1.75 m in extent, will support
L1617: between 440 and 475 W of spacecraft load power during the cruise phase and 650 W during the orbit at Mercury. The
L1618: panels themselves produce more than 2 kW of power near Mercury, but to prevent stress on MESSENGER’s electronics
L1619: and keep operating temperatures within acceptable limits, onboard power processors convert only what the spacecraft
L1620: was designed to consume in orbit.
L1621: The custom-developed panels are two-thirds mirrors (called optical solar reflectors) and one-third triple-junction solar
L1622: cells, which convert 28% of the sunlight hitting them into electricity. Each panel has two rows of mirrors for every row
L1623: of cells; the small mirrors reflect the Sun’s energy and keep the panel cooler. The panels also rotate, so the operations
L1624: team tilts the panels away from the Sun, positioning them to get the required power while maintaining a normal surface
L1625: operating temperature of about 150° C.
L1626: Propulsion
L1627: MESSENGER’s dual-mode propulsion system includes a 660-N bipropellant thruster for large maneuvers and 16
L1628: hydrazine-propellant thrusters for smaller trajectory adjustments and attitude control. The Large Velocity Adjust (LVA)
L1629: thruster requires a combination of hydrazine fuel and nitrogen tetroxide oxidizer. Fuel and oxidizer are stored in customdesigned, lightweight titanium tanks integrated into the spacecraft’s composite frame. Helium pressurizes the system
L1630: and pushes the fuel and oxidizer through to the engines.
L1631: At launch the spacecraft carried just under 600 kg of propellant, and it will use nearly 30% of it during the maneuver
L1632: that inserts the spacecraft into orbit around Mercury. The hydrazine thrusters play several important roles: four 22-N
L1633: thrusters are used for small course corrections and help steady MESSENGER during large engine burns. The dozen 4.4-N
L1634: thrusters are also used for small course corrections and also help steady the spacecraft during all propulsive maneuvers.
L1635: These smallest thrusters can also serve as a backup for the reaction wheels that maintain the spacecraft’s orientation
L1636: during normal cruise and orbital operations.
L1637: Communications
L1638: MESSENGER’s X-band coherent communications system includes two high-gain, electronically steered, phased-array
L1639: antennas — the first ever used on a deep-space mission; two medium-gain fanbeam antennas; and four low-gain
L1640: antennas. The circularly polarized phased arrays — developed by APL and located with the fanbeam antennas on the
L1641: front and back of the spacecraft — are the main link for sending science data back to Earth. For better reliability in the
L1642: high-temperature environment the antennas are fixed; they “point” electronically across a 45° field of regard without
L1643: moving parts.
L1644: High-gain antennas send radio signals through a narrower, more concentrated beam than medium- or low-gain
L1645: antennas and are used to send large amounts of data over the same distance as a lower-gain antenna. The fanbeam
L1646: antennas, also located on MESSENGER’s front and back sides, are used for lower-rate data transmissions to the Earth
L1647: as well as nominal command transmissions from the Earth, such as operating commands, status data, or emergency
L1648: communications. The four low-gain antennas provide hemispheric fields of view from the top, bottom, front, and
L1649: back of the spacecraft, providing primarily an emergency bi-directional communications link with the Earth in the
L1650: event attitude knowledge is lost. MESSENGER’s downlink rate ranges from 9.9 bits per second to 104 kilobits per
L1651: second; operators can send commands at 7.8 to 500 bits per second. Transmission rates vary according to onboard
L1652: communications system configuration, spacecraft distance from the Earth, and ground-station antenna size.

L1654: Command and Data Handling
L1655: MESSENGER’s “brain” is its Integrated Electronics Module (IEM), a space- and weight-saving device that combines
L1656: the spacecraft’s core avionics into a single box. The spacecraft carries a pair of identical IEMs for backup purposes; both
L1657: house a 25-MHz main processor and 10-MHz fault-protection processor. All four are radiation-hardened RAD6000
L1658: processors, based on predecessors of the PowerPC chip found in some models of home computers. The computers,
L1659: slow by current home-computer standards, are state of the art for the radiation tolerance required on the MESSENGER
L1660: mission.
L1661: Programmed to monitor the condition of MESSENGER’s key systems, both fault-protection processors are turned
L1662: on at all times and protect the spacecraft by turning off components and/or switching to backup components when
L1663: necessary. The main processor runs the command and data handling software for data transfer and file storage, as well
L1664: as the guidance and control software used to navigate and point the spacecraft. Each IEM also includes a solid-state
L1665: data recorder, power converters, and the interfaces between the processors and MESSENGER’s instruments and systems.
L1666: Intricate flight software executes MESSENGER’s Command and Data Handling system. MESSENGER receives operating
L1667: commands from Earth and can perform them in real time or store them for later execution. Most of the frequent,
L1668: critical operations (such as propulsive maneuvers) are programmed into the flight computer’s memory and timed to run
L1669: automatically.
L1670: For data, MESSENGER carries two solid-state recorders (one backup) able to store up to 1 gigabyte each. The main
L1671: processor collects, compresses, and stores images and other data from the subsystems and instruments onto the
L1672: recorder; the software sorts the data into files in a manner similar to how files are stored on a PC. The main processor
L1673: selects the files with highest priority to transmit to Earth, or mission operators can download data files in any order the
L1674: team chooses.
L1675: In orbit around Mercury, data downlink rates will vary predominantly with spacecraft-to-Earth distance. Thus when
L1676: orbiting Mercury, MESSENGER will store most of its data when it’s farther from Earth, typically sending only information
L1677: on its condition and the highest-priority images and measurements during contacts through NASA’s Deep Space
L1678: Network. The spacecraft will send most of the recorded data when Mercury’s path around the Sun brings it closer to
L1679: Earth.
L1680: Guidance and Control
L1681: MESSENGER is well protected against the heat, but it must always know its orientation relative to Mercury, Earth,
L1682: and the Sun and be “smart” enough to keep its sunshade pointed at the Sun. Attitude determination — knowing in
L1683: which direction MESSENGER is facing — is performed using star-tracking cameras, digital Sun sensors, and an inertial
L1684: measurement unit (IMU), which contains gyroscopes and accelerometers). Attitude control for the three-axis stabilized
L1685: craft is accomplished using four internal reaction wheels and, when necessary, MESSENGER’s small thrusters.
L1686: The IMU accurately determines the spacecraft’s rotation rate, and MESSENGER tracks its own orientation by checking
L1687: the location of stars and the Sun. Star-tracking cameras on MESSENGER’s top deck store a complete map of the
L1688: heavens; once a second, one of the cameras takes a wide-angle picture of space, compares the locations of stars to its
L1689: onboard map, and then calculates the spacecraft’s orientation. The guidance and control software also automatically
L1690: rotates the solar panels to the commanded Sun-relative orientation, as the spacecraft body rotates, ensuring that the
L1691: panels produce sufficient power while maintaining safe temperatures.
L1692: The suite of Sun sensors backs up the star trackers, continuously measuring MESSENGER’s angle to the Sun. If the
L1693: flight software detects that the Sun is “moving” out of a designated safe zone, it can initiate an automatic turn to
L1694: ensure that the shade faces the Sun. Ground controllers can then analyze the situation while the spacecraft turns its
L1695: antennas to Earth and awaits instructions — an operating condition known as “safe” mode.

L1784: Mission Summary
L1785: Cruise trajectory
L1786: The MESSENGER mission takes advantage of an ingenious
L1787: trajectory design, lightweight materials, and miniaturization
L1788: of electronics, all developed in the three decades since
L1789: Mariner 10 flew past Mercury in 1974 and 1975. The
L1790: compact orbiter, fortified against the searing conditions
L1791: near the Sun, will investigate key questions about Mercury’s
L1792: characteristics and environment with a set of seven scientific
L1793: instruments.
L1794: On a 7.9-billion-km journey that included more than
L1795: 15 loops	around	the	Sun,	the	spacecraft’ s	trajectory	included
L1796: one pass by Earth, two by Venus, and three by Mercury,
L1797: before a propulsive burn will ease it into orbit around its
L1798: target planet. The Earth flyby in August 2005, along with
L1799: the Venus flybys in October 2006 and June 2007 and the
L1800: three Mercury flybys in January 2008, October 2008, and
L1801: September 2009 used the pull of each planet’s gravity to
L1802: guide MESSENGER toward Mercury’s orbit.
L1803: The combined effect of the six gravity assists from three
L1804: planets and five deterministic deep-space maneuvers (DSMs)
L1805: — using the bipropellant Large Velocity Adjust (LVA) engine
L1806: of the spacecraft and the influence of the Sun — accelerated
L1807: the spacecraft from an average speed around the Sun of
L1808: 30 km/s	(the	Earth’ s	average	speed	around	the	Sun)	to
L1809: 48 km/s	(Mercury’ s	average	speed	around	the	Sun).
L1810: The cruise phase of the mission concludes in March 2011,
L1811: when the spacecraft will execute the Mercury orbit insertion
L1812: (MOI) maneuver, slowing the spacecraft and allowing it to
L1813: be captured into orbit around Mercury.
L1814: Getting a Boost
L1815: For a gravity assist, a spacecraft flies close to
L1816: a planet and trades with the planet’s orbital
L1817: momentum around the Sun. Depending on the
L1818: relative difference in mass between the planet
L1819: and the spacecraft, as well as the distance
L1820: between the two, this exchange of momentum
L1821: can impart a substantial change in spacecraft
L1822: speed. Since the spacecraft’s mass is negligible
L1823: compared with that of the planet, this process
L1824: has a negligible effect on the planet’s orbit
L1825: around the Sun. But the spacecraft receives a
L1826: great boost on the way to its next destination.
L1827: Gravity-assist maneuvers can be used to speed
L1828: a spacecraft up or slow a spacecraft down.
L1829: Closest approach distance, direction, and the
L1830: velocity of a spacecraft relative to the planet’s
L1831: velocity all affect the acceleration magnitude and
L1832: direction change of the spacecraft’s trajectory.
L1833: The greatest change in a spacecraft’s speed and
L1834: direction occurs when a slow-moving spacecraft
L1835: approaches just above the surface or cloud
L1836: tops of a massive planet. The least change in a
L1837: spacecraft’s speed and direction occurs when
L1838: a fast-moving spacecraft approaches a small
L1839: planet from a great distance.

L1844: Multiple Flybys
L1845: Mariner 10 flew past Venus to reach Mercury, but the idea of multiple Venus/Mercury flybys to help
L1846: a spacecraft “catch” Mercury and begin orbiting the planet came years later, when Chen-wan Yen of
L1847: NASA’s Jet Propulsion Laboratory developed the concept in the mid-1980s. MESSENGER adopted this
L1848: mission design approach; without these flybys, MESSENGER would move so fast past Mercury that no
L1849: existing propulsion system could slow it down sufficiently for it to be captured into orbit.

L1851: Launch
L1852: MESSENGER launched from Pad B of Space Launch Complex 17 at Cape Canaveral Air Force Station, Fla., on a threestage Boeing Delta II expendable launch vehicle on August 3, 2004. The Delta II 7925H-9.5 (heavy lift) model was the
L1853: largest allowed for NASA Discovery missions. It features a liquid-fueled first stage with nine strap-on solid boosters, a
L1854: second-stage liquid-fueled engine, and a third-stage solid-fuel rocket. With MESSENGER secured in a 9.5-m fairing on
L1855: top, the launch vehicle was about 40 m tall.
L1856: The launch vehicle imparted an excess launch energy per mass (usually denoted by C3 and equal to the excess over
L1857: what is required for Earth escape) of approximately 16.4 km2/s2 to the spacecraft, setting up the spacecraft for a return
L1858: pass by the Earth approximately one year from launch.
L1859: Earth flyby highlights
L1860: MESSENGER swung by its home planet on August 2, 2005, for a gravity assist that propelled it deeper into the inner
L1861: Solar System. MESSENGER’s systems performed flawlessly as the spacecraft swooped around Earth, coming to a closest
L1862: approach point of about 2,348 km over central Mongolia at 3:13 p.m. EDT. The spacecraft used the tug of Earth’s
L1863: gravity to change its trajectory significantly, bringing its average orbital distance nearly 29 million km closer to the Sun
L1864: and sending it toward Venus for gravity assists in 2006 and 2007.
L1865: Team members in the MESSENGER Mission Operations Center at APL watch the spacecraft launch from Cape
L1866: Canaveral. The team began operating the spacecraft less than an hour later, after MESSENGER separated
L1867: from the launch vehicle.

L1902: Venus gravity assists
L1903: MESSENGER has flown by Venus twice using the tug of the planet’s gravity to change its trajectory, to shrink the
L1904: spacecraft’s orbit around the Sun, and to bring it closer to Mercury.
L1905: During the first Venus flyby on October 24, 2006, the spacecraft came within 2,987 km of the surface of Venus.
L1906: Shortly before the encounter, MESSENGER entered superior solar conjunction, where it was on the opposite side of the
L1907: Sun from Earth and during which reliable communication between MESSENGER and mission operators was not possible.
L1908: In addition, during the flyby the spacecraft experienced the mission’s first and longest eclipse of the Sun by a planet.
L1909: During the eclipse, which lasted approximately 56 minutes, the spacecraft’s solar arrays were in the shadow of Venus
L1910: and MESSENGER operated on battery power.
L1911: MESSENGER swung by Venus for the second time on June 5, 2007, speeding over the planet’s cloud tops at a
L1912: relative velocity of more than 48,000 km/hour and passing within 338 km of its surface near the boundary between the
L1913: lowland plains of Rusalka Planitia and the rifted uplands of Aphrodite Terra. The maneuver sharpened the spacecraft’s
L1914: aim toward the first encounter with Mercury and presented a special opportunity to calibrate several of its science
L1915: instruments and learn something new about Earth’s nearest neighbor.

L1954: Flying by Mercury
L1955: On January 14, 2008, at 19:04:39 UTC (2:04:39 p.m. EST) the MESSENGER spacecraft executed its first Mercury flyby,
L1956: passing over the uncharted surface of the planet at an altitude of 201.4 km, an even more accurate aim than for the
L1957: second Venus flyby. The primary purpose of this activity was to shrink the orbital period of the spacecraft around the
L1958: Sun by 11 days, bringing MESSENGER’s orbit closer to Mercury’s orbit. On October 6, 2008, at 08:40:22 UTC (4:40:22
L1959: EDT) the MESSENGER spacecraft executed its second Mercury flyby, passing above the surface at an altitude of 199.2
L1960: km, within a phenomenal 760 meters of the planned flyby altitude! Although the flyby enabled direct observation of
L1961: additional previously unobserved planetary surface features, the primary purpose of the flyby was to shrink the orbital
L1962: period of the spacecraft around the Sun by an additional 16 days and increase the ecliptic inclination of the spacecraft
L1963: by 0.1 degrees, further matching the orbit of Mercury around the Sun. The third and final Mercury flyby on September
L1964: 29, 2009, at 21:54:56 UTC (5:54:56 p.m. EST) further decreased the spacecraft’s orbital period around the Sun by
L1965: almost 13 days to 104.8 days, and increased the inclination of the spacecraft’s orbit by a tiny amount (0.001º) such that
L1966: it now closely matches Mercury’s 7.0 degree orbit inclination. The spacecraft’s lowest altitude above the planet was
L1967: 227.5 kilometers. These adjustments to the spacecraft’s orbit will enable it to enter orbit about Mercury on March 18,
L1968: 2011 UTC.

L1987: MESSENGER’s deep-space maneuvers
L1988: In conjunction with the six planetary flybys, MESSENGER’s complex 6.6-year cruise trajectory has included more than
L1989: 40 anticipated trajectory-correction maneuvers (TCMs). These TCMs included five deterministic deep-space maneuvers
L1990: (DSMs), which used the spacecraft’s bipropellant Large Velocity Adjust (LVA) engine. In addition to imparting a combined
L1991: spacecraft change in velocity of more than 1 km/s, the DSMs were the primary method used to target the spacecraft
L1992: before each planetary flyby (except the second Venus flyby). Smaller velocity-adjustment maneuvers that used the
L1993: propulsion system’s monopropellant thrusters fine-tuned the trajectory between the main DSMs and the gravity-assist
L1994: flybys of the planets.
L1995: On December 12, 2005, MESSENGER successfully fired its bipropellant LVA engine for the first time, completing the
L1996: first of the five critical deep-space maneuvers (DSM-1). The maneuver, just over 8 minutes long, changed MESSENGER’s
L1997: speed by approximately 316 m/s, placing the spacecraft on target for the first Venus flyby on October 24, 2006.
L1998: This maneuver was the first to rely solely on the LVA, the largest and most efficient engine of the propulsion system.
L1999: Maneuvers performed with the LVA use about 30% less total propellant mass — both fuel and oxidizer — than the
L2000: other thrusters, which use monopropellant fuel only. Approximately 100 kg of propellant (both fuel and oxidizer), about
L2001: 18% of the total onboard propellant, was used to complete DSM-1.
L2002: On October 17, 2007, MESSENGER completed its second critical DSM — 250 million km from Earth — successfully
L2003: firing the LVA again to change the spacecraft’s trajectory and target it for its historic flyby of Mercury on January 14,
L2004: 2008. The maneuver, just over 5 minutes long, consumed approximately 70 kg of propellant (both fuel and oxidizer),
L2005: changing the velocity of the spacecraft by approximately 226 m/s.
L2006: On March 19, 2008, MESSENGER completed its third critical DSM, successfully firing the LVA once again to change
L2007: the spacecraft’s trajectory and target it for the second flyby of Mercury on October 6, 2008. The shortest deterministic
L2008: maneuver for the mission on the LVA, just over 2.5 minutes long, consumed approximately 21 kg of propellant (both
L2009: fuel and oxidizer), changing the velocity of the spacecraft by approximately 72 m/s.
L2010: On December 4 and 8, 2008, MESSENGER completed its fourth critical DSM, successfully firing the LVA twice to
L2011: adjust the spacecraft’s trajectory and target it for the third flyby of Mercury on September 29, 2009. This maneuver
L2012: was purposely split into two parts to provide engineers a practice opportunity for the cruise-ending Mercury Orbit
L2013: Insertion maneuver. Combined, the two parts of this DSM consumed about 68 kg of propellant over a total firing time
L2014: of	6.5 minutes,	changing	the	spacecraft	velocity	by	222	m/s	and	then	by	25	m/s,	respectively,	for	each	part	of	the
L2015: maneuver.
L2016: MESSENGER completed its fifth and final critical DSM on November 24, 2009. This maneuver, which lasted
L2017: 4.6 minutes,	changed	the	spacecraft’ s	velocity	by	178	m/s	and	used	approximately	46	kg	of	propellant.

L2140: NASA Discovery Program
L2141: MESSENGER is the seventh mission in NASA’s Discovery Program of lower-cost, highly focused, planetary science
L2142: investigations. Created in 1992, Discovery challenges teams of scientists and engineers to find innovative and
L2143: imaginative ways to uncover the mysteries of the Solar System within limited, cost-capped budgets and schedules.
L2144: Other Discovery missions
L2145: NEAR (Near Earth Asteroid Rendezvous) marked the Discovery Program’s first launch, in February 1996. The NEAR
L2146: Shoemaker spacecraft became the first to orbit an asteroid when it reached 433 Eros in February 2000. After collecting
L2147: 10 times the data initially expected during a year around Eros, in February 2001, NEAR Shoemaker became the first
L2148: spacecraft to land on an asteroid and collect data from its surface.
L2149: Mars Pathfinder launched December 1996 and landed on Mars in July 1997. The mission demonstrated several
L2150: tools and techniques for future Mars missions — such as entering, descending, and landing with airbags to deliver a
L2151: robotic rover — while captivating the world with color pictures from the red planet.
L2152: Lunar Prospector orbited Earth’s Moon for 18 months after launching in January 1998. The mission’s data enabled
L2153: scientists to create detailed maps of the gravity, magnetic properties, and chemical makeup of the Moon’s entire surface.
L2154: Stardust, launched in February 1999, collected samples of comet dust and provided the closest look yet at a comet
L2155: nucleus when it sailed through the coma of Wild 2 in January 2004. It returned the cometary dust to Earth in January
L2156: 2006.
L2157: Genesis, launched in August 2001, collected solar wind particles and returned them to Earth in September 2004.
L2158: The samples are improving our understanding of the isotopic composition of the Sun, information that will help to
L2159: identify what the young Solar System was like.
L2160: CONTOUR (Comet Nucleus Tour) was designed to fly past and study at least two very different comets as they visited
L2161: the inner Solar System. The spacecraft was lost six weeks after launch, during a critical rocket-firing maneuver in August
L2162: 2002 to boost it from Earth’s orbit onto a comet-chasing path around the Sun.
L2163: Deep Impact, launched in January 2005, was the first experiment to probe beneath the surface of a comet,
L2164: attempting to reveal never-before-seen materials that would provide clues to the internal composition and structure
L2165: of a comet. In July 2005, a variety of instruments, both onboard the spacecraft and at ground-based and space-based
L2166: observatories around the world, observed the impact with the comet and examined the resulting debris and interior
L2167: material.
L2168: 
L2169: Dawn, launched in September 2007 toward Vesta and Ceres, two of the largest main-belt asteroids in our Solar
L2170: System, will provide key data on asteroid properties by orbiting and observing these minor planets. Dawn is scheduled to
L2171: arrive at Vesta in July 2011.
L2172: Kepler, launched in March 2009, is monitoring 100,000 stars similar to our Sun for four years, using new technology
L2173: to search local regions of the galaxy for Earth-size (or smaller) planets for the first time.
L2174: The GRAIL (Gravity Recovery and Interior Laboratory) mission, scheduled to launch in September 2011, will fly twin
L2175: spacecraft in tandem orbits around the Moon for several months to measure its gravity field in unprecedented detail.
L2176: The mission also will answer longstanding questions about Earth’s Moon and provide scientists a better understanding of
L2177: how Earth and other rocky planets in the Solar System formed.
L2178: Discovery also includes Missions of Opportunity — not complete Discovery missions, but pieces of a larger NASA or
L2179: non-NASA mission or creative reuses of spacecraft that have completed their prime missions. Those selected to date for
L2180: flight include:
L2181: •	 The	ASPERA-3 (Analyzer of Space Plasma and Energetic Atoms) instrument is studying the interaction between the
L2182: solar wind and the Martian atmosphere from the European Space Agency’s Mars Express spacecraft, which began
L2183: orbiting Mars in December 2003.
L2184: •	 The	M3 (Moon Mineralogy Mapper), pronounced M-cubed, is one of eleven instruments that flew onboard
L2185: Chandrayaan-1, which launched in October 2008. Chandrayaan-1, India’s first deep space mission, was a project of
L2186: the Indian Space Research Organisation (ISRO). The goals of the mission included expanding scientific knowledge of
L2187: the Moon, upgrading India’s technological capability, and providing challenging opportunities for planetary research
L2188: for the younger generation.
L2189: •	 The	EPOXI mission combines two science investigations — the Extrasolar Planet Observation and Characterization
L2190: (EPOCh) and the Deep Impact Extended Investigation (DIXI). Both investigations are using the Deep Impact spacecraft,
L2191: which finished its prime mission in 2005. EPOCh is using the Deep Impact spacecraft to observe several nearby bright
L2192: stars for transits by orbiting planets, and DIXI involved the successful flyby of comet Hartley 2 in October 2010.
L2193: •	 NExT (New Exploration of Tempel 1) reused NASA’s Stardust spacecraft to revisit comet Tempel 1, the cometary
L2194: target of Deep Impact, in February 2011. This investigation provided the first look at the changes to a comet nucleus
L2195: produced after its close approach to the Sun.
L2196: •	 STROFIO (Start from a ROtating FIeld mass spectrOmeter) is a mass spectrometer that is part of the SERENA (Search
L2197: Exospheric Refilling and Emitted Natural Abundances) instrument package selected to fly on the European Space
L2198: Agency’s BepiColombo Mercury Planetary Orbiter spacecraft, scheduled to launch in 2014. The SERENA instrument
L2199: has two neutral particle analyzers (STROFIO and ELENA) and two ion spectrometers (MICA and PICAM). STROFIO,
L2200: from the Greek word “strofi” (to rotate), will determine the composition of Mercury’s exosphere.
